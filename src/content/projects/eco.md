<div class="highlight">
    <p><strong>Problem statement:</strong> How might we develop sustainable & effective sound baffles using low-carbon composites that can be scaled for industrial production?</p>
</div>

As an extension of our Structures and Materials course, my team and I developed a process plan for a biodegradable sound-absorbing composite using coconut coir and gum Arabic with a team of 4 other students to be used as alternatives to synthetic sound baffles.

Coconut coir was chosen as the base material due to its porosity and low density, making it an ideal biodegradable sound adsorption material. 

We were faced with limited commercially available options for a completely biodegradable binder. We first tested a mixture of gelatin and glycerol. However, the end product was very dense and remained most after curing initial curing, resulting in mold. We then pivoted to gum Arabic. The resulting composite was more porous, stiffer and dried easier, making gum Arabic our binder of choice.

We bought processed coconut coir mats and cut them into 15cmx10cm sheets. 6 sheets were stacked on top of each other. Between each layer, a coating of gum arabic was applied. The stacks were then placed into rectangular moulds to press the stack flat. The moulds were fabricated from plywood. Once the moulds were secure, the samples were placed in an oven at 80°C for 2 days. Samples of different gum-Arabic-to-water ratios were made to investigate the effects of the composition of the binder on the composite's properties.

<br />

## Testing
**1. Tensile Testing**

As the fabrication and testing lead, I conducted the tensile testing for material characterization to measure the composites strength and hardness. The samples were cut into dog bone shapes using a bandsaw. The stress-strain curve was obtained using a Zwick Roell tensile testing machine.

The 1:2 gum Arabic to water sample showed the best strength with both a high Young's modulus and high ultimate tensile strength. The interlocking fibres of the coconut coir give the material its strength. The fibres also prevents sudden failure. The gradual failure of the material makes it safer as cracks do not propagate easily.

<br />

**2. Sound Adsorption Testing**

We fabricated a rudimentary impedance test rig using PVC pipes and a white-noise-generating circuit to quantify the composite's sound-absorption. The control adsorption surface was a concrete puck to mimic concrete walls. A plywood sample was also prepared as a point of comparison.

The samples were placed on one end of the tube while the speaker played white noise. 2 uMik microphones measured the intensity of the sound across a range of 20Hz to 20kHz. The data was processed in Adobe Audition and the average intensity was calculated to reduce influences of nodal points.
